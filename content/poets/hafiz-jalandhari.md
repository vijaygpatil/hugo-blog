## The Man

Abu al-Asar Hafeez Jalandhari was born on January 14, 1900, in Jalandhar, in what is now Punjab, India. He adopted the pen name *Hafiz* — a title that in the Islamic tradition denotes one who has memorised the Quran — and it carried both devotional weight and the shadow of Hafiz Shirazi, the great fourteenth-century Persian poet whose own pen name he was invoking. He made no effort to escape the comparison; he invited the inheritance.

He grew up in the literary culture of undivided Punjab, where Urdu and Punjabi poetry existed in close proximity and the *mushaira* tradition — the gathering of poets before live audiences — was a living institution rather than a preserved one. He was largely self-educated in the literary sense, shaped more by the tradition he read than by formal instruction, and he entered public literary life as a young man with a voice already recognisably his own.

His early collections established him as a lyric poet of considerable emotional range. He wrote ghazals, nazms, and patriotic verse, and he was a participant in the cultural ferment of the years leading up to Partition. When Pakistan came into existence in 1947, he was already one of the most prominent Urdu poets in the new country's cultural landscape.

In 1952, he was commissioned to write the national anthem of Pakistan. *Pak sar zameen shad baad* — the anthem he produced — is still in use today: a short, formally elevated text in Persian-inflected Urdu, the kind of verse that sounds like it has always existed. It is among the most recognised things he ever wrote and, in the way of such works, it has largely eclipsed everything else.

He died on January 21, 1982, in Lahore, having spent the last decades of his life as a major figure in Pakistani cultural institutions.

## The Poetry

Hafiz Jalandhari occupies a peculiar position in the Urdu tradition. He is among its most *cited* poets — millions of people in Pakistan know at least one line of his work, even if they do not know his name — and yet his ghazals, the more interior work, are less widely discussed than those of his contemporaries.

His ghazals belong to a vein of classical Urdu verse that works through careful accumulation rather than sudden compression. He was not a poet of the single devastating couplet in the way Ghalib was, or of the politically charged lyric beauty of Faiz. His strength was the sustained mood — the ghazal as a structure that builds, sher by sher, a single emotional state from different angles until it is fully inhabited.

His major collections include *Naghmaat-e-Hafiz*, *Sar-o-Saamaan*, and *Naghma-e-Zaar*. He also wrote epic verse, most notably *Shahnama-e-Islam*, an ambitious retelling of Islamic history in the tradition of the Persian *shahnamahs*.

## The Themes

**Absence as the permanent condition:** Hafiz's ghazals return again and again to the speaker's exclusion — from the beloved's gathering, from the beloved's attention, from the world's understanding. He is not writing about the pain of losing love so much as the strange, habitual state of living alongside that loss.

**The world's ignorance as kindness:** In several of his ghazals, the people of the world are not condemned for their lack of sympathy but gently excused — they are simply not *mahram*, not privy to the inner state. This is an unusual generosity that runs through his work: bitterness withheld, blame redirected inward.

**Asymmetry between lover and beloved:** His speakers are almost always aware that what they feel is not matched. The beloved is not cruel — cruelty would imply investment. The beloved simply does not feel the same weight. This asymmetry, named without self-pity, is one of the distinctive qualities of his emotional register.

**The physiology of grief:** Hafiz writes about grief as something that happens in the body — tears that do or do not come, a flood building inside when it cannot flow out, the encounter that does not heal what separation built up. He is attentive to how grief operates as a physical state, not merely a psychological one.

## His Language

Hafiz wrote in a Persianised Urdu that draws on classical compound constructions — *sharik-e-girya-e-shabnam*, *chashm-e-karam*, *dida-e-pur-nam* — without becoming opaque. His imagery is drawn from the classical vocabulary: flowers, dew, the cage, the gathering, the eye of grace. What he does with these inherited materials is quieter than what his more celebrated contemporaries did with them: he does not set them against political conditions or push them toward the cosmological. He uses them to map the interior very carefully.

He is among the Urdu poets whose work has been set to music, and several of his ghazals have been recorded by classical vocalists. The musical settings have helped some of his verse reach audiences who came to it through listening rather than reading.

## Why He Endures

The national anthem is the obvious answer, and it is also the wrong one. The anthem endures because nations need anthems. The ghazals endure for a different reason.

Hafiz Jalandhari's interior work endures because it is honest about the minor key of love — not its intensity at the beginning, not its devastation at the end, but the long middle state in which you are simply absent from somewhere you want to be, and life proceeds there without you, and the world does not know, and even if you met by chance it would not reduce what absence has accumulated. This is not dramatic grief. It is something quieter and more persistent, and he maps it with a precision that earns his readers' recognition.
